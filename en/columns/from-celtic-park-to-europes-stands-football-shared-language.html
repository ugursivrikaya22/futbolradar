From Celtic Park to Europe's Stands: Football's Shared Language Spoke at 3-2

FutbolRadar 06 November 2026

Avrupa'daki ekran da tribündür: Celtic Park 3-2'sinden diasporaya sesleniş.

Share this column
fShare on Facebook XShare on X

This is an opinion column. It reflects the writer's personal view and should not be confused with FutbolRadar's verified match data.

An evening in Europe. A room in Berlin, a café in Rotterdam, a pub in London, a kitchen table in Vienna. The screen is the same; the heart is beating somewhere else. At Celtic Park the whistle went and, in matchday four of the UEFA Europa League league phase, Celtic beat Beşiktaş 3-2.[1][2][3] This column is not here to make you memorize a scoreline. It is here to speak to you — wherever in Europe you watched that score unfold.

This take: Football's shared language is not spoken in possession percentages. It is spoken in two stands rising at the same second, in the same held breath.

Five goals at Celtic Park, one language

Half-time finished 0-0. All five goals arrived in the second half.[1][2] Across Anadolu Ajansı, CNN Türk and BBC Sport the order was this: Junior Olaïtan at 54' (0-1), Callum Osmand at 62' (1-1), Camilo Durán at 78' (2-1), Orkun Kökçü at 86' (2-2), Durán again at 87' (3-2).[1][2][3] The penalty came in the 52nd minute after Lotomba's challenge on Poku; Johnstone saved it, and Olaïtan finished the rebound.[1][2]

BBC match statistics put the ball with Beşiktaş: possession 38.5% to 61.5%, shots 10 to 15, touches in the opposition box 13 to 32.[1] The side that wrote the scoreline was Celtic, who threw on Osmand, Durán and Donovan together around the 61st minute.[1] Beşiktaş took their first league-phase defeat and stayed on 7 points in 8th. Celtic climbed to 3 points in 32nd.[1][3]

This take: For anyone watching from any city in Europe that night, the real sentence was not the final score. It was this: the side that holds the ball and the side that holds the score are not always the same — and that tension feels identical in every language.

The diaspora stand: there is a voice outside the stadium too

Celtic Park's grass is in Glasgow. The sound of that night did not stay in Scotland. For a Turkish football fan living in Europe, a Beşiktaş away night is not only a result line; it is belonging, live. Nostalgia here is not an old photograph. Nostalgia is converting kick-off to your local clock, the group chat lighting up, the room turning into a stand the moment the net bulges.

The referee was Germany's Sascha Stegemann.[2][3] That detail looks like cold match notes. For the spectator in Europe it opens another door: the person who blows the whistle, the side that scores, and the person watching the screen are on the same continent. Football does not draw a border between Türkiye and Europe; it builds a bridge. One end is under the lights at Tüpraş, the other is in the roar of Celtic Park, and the middle is your living room.

This take: The diaspora stand does not tear tickets; but after full time it carries the lump in the throat longer than the home end does.

The bond between Europe and Türkiye: the shared language speaks in goals

In our pre-match column we said the real match would begin in the 46th minute. The result confirmed that warning: 0-0 at the break, then chaos.[1][2] According to BBC Sport, Celtic's equaliser arrived 43 seconds after Osmand came on.[1] Kökçü returned from injury and scored; Durán's strike at 87' made that goal not enough.[1][2][3]

This is where the Europe–Türkiye bond becomes concrete. On one side Glasgow's green-and-white chorus; on the other a black-and-white away hope; and between them something beyond statistics: people celebrating and collapsing in the same second. Orkun's shot lifts a room in Berlin and a room in Bursa at the same instant. Durán's 87th sits both London and Beşiktaş down together.

For form and the European picture see the Beşiktaş team page, Beşiktaş form guide and our post-match column. The columns index is the archive of that bond.

This take: A European night is not only a points calculation for clubs. It is the overnight exam of the shared language spoken between a continent and a country.

This site is the table for that shared language

To everyone reading futbolradar.com in Turkish or English — especially spectators watching from Europe — I want to say this: you are not far away. Part of matchday traffic comes from your clock, your screen, your longing. That is why this piece was thought in two languages — because the language spoken at Celtic Park and the breath held in your kitchen belong to the same match.

On the BBC table Beşiktaş are still inside the automatic last-16 band; Celtic took their first three points of the phase that night.[1] The story is not over. It will not be. As Europa League weeks return, the question returns with them: who has the ball, who has the score, and where is the stand?

This take: When we address football spectators in Europe, we are not drilling scorelines. We are reminding you of belonging. Because in this game the thing that lasts longest is not three points won; it is the language shared.

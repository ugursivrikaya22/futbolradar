The Whistle Blows, but Trust Has Been Silenced

FutbolRadar 05 October 2026

Opinion / Referees | Trust in the institution of refereeing and the need for provable transparency

This is an opinion column. It reflects the writer's personal view and should not be confused with FutbolRadar's verified match data.

Translated from the Turkish original: Read the original column in Turkish

For years in Turkish football, we have talked about referees.

An offside line, a penalty not given, a card not shown, why VAR didn't intervene…

After games we watched the same incidents over and over for hours.

But today what we are talking about is no longer a refereeing mistake.

Today what we are talking about is trust in the institution of refereeing in Turkish football itself.

In the investigation being conducted in Istanbul, known to the public as the "Referees File", arrest orders were issued for five suspects, among them Ferhat Gündoğdu, who resigned as chairman of the Central Referee Committee (MHK). The investigation covers allegations of forgery of official documents, breach of trust, unlawful acquisition of personal data and other offences.

And the file has not been closed.

On the morning of 5 October, Süper Lig referee Yasin Kol and assistant referee Göktuğ Hazar Erel were also referred to the Istanbul Courthouse after their processing at the police station.

Here it is vital to hold a very important line:

Being investigated, detained or referred to the courthouse does not mean that a crime has been established.

The presumption of innocence applies to everyone until a court has ruled.

But there is another question the football public needs to ask:

How did we get here?

Because the issue is no longer just about what a few people did.

The issue is what the child watching the game in the stands, the fan in front of the television, the footballer sweating on the pitch and an entire football nation feel when they look at a referee.

When the referee blows the whistle, everyone has to believe the same thing:

Behind that whistle there are only the laws of the game.

Nothing else.

No connections.

No pressure.

No favouritism.

No personal agendas.

And no other order set up behind closed doors.

Because if you think the capital of the refereeing profession is money, you are wrong.

The real capital of refereeing is trust.

And once trust is lost, you cannot bring it back with VAR recordings, press conferences or statements from a new committee.

When a fan starts to doubt, football changes.

Even when the referee makes the right decision, they wonder "why did he give that?"

When he makes a wrong decision, they ask "was that a coincidence?"

An appointment is made, and people start researching the referee's past before the game.

That is the real disaster.

Because for football to survive, people have to believe that the result of the game is decided on the pitch.

That is why today's developments are not just a matter for the corridors of the courthouse.

This is perhaps one of the most important tests of trust Turkish football has faced in many years.

And the picture is not limited to referees either.

The TFF recently referred hundreds of current and former club officials to disciplinary proceedings as part of its betting inquiries. According to a Reuters report dated 1 October, 448 officials were sent to the disciplinary board under interim measures.

So the question now is not:

"Did a referee make a mistake?"

The question is:

How will we trust the institutions of Turkish football again?

From this point on, what needs to be done is clear.

Whoever is guilty must answer for it within the law.

Whoever is innocent must have their name and reputation cleared just as openly.

The federation must not sweep anything under the carpet.

Referee appointments, assessments, promotions and disciplinary processes must be made as transparent as possible.

Because what Turkish football needs today is not a new slogan.

Nor a new committee.

And certainly not another "clean slate" statement.

What Turkish football needs is provable transparency.

Perhaps for years we were looking in the wrong place.

Every week we argued about the referee's final whistle.

Yet the real problem may have started before the first whistle.

Now Turkish football has a very heavy but very valuable opportunity in front of it.

If these investigations really are seen through to the end, if the same law is applied to everyone and the system is made more transparent, this dark period could be the beginning of a clean-up.

But if it is left half-done…

Names will be forgotten.

Files will be closed.

Chairmen will change.

Referees will go, and new ones will come.

But that small doubt planted in the fans' minds will remain.

And there is nothing more dangerous for football than that.

Because what keeps football alive is not just goals.

Not the shirts.

Not the multi-million transfers.

What keeps football alive is everyone being able to believe, at the end of ninety minutes:

"The pitch decided the winner."

If Turkish football cannot rebuild that sentence…

What we lose will be far bigger than a few games.

What we lose will be football itself.
